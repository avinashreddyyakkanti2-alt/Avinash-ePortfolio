Reflection

What this module taught me - and why it matters beyond the classroom

Looking back on the module

This module turned out to be one of the most practically useful parts of my MSc so far. Going in, I thought a “portfolio module” would be lightweight - something to polish a CV. Coming out, I realise how much of what we covered maps directly onto how real corporate teams actually work: version control, reproducible reporting, personal branding, and professional web presence.

The sessions were consistently engaging, and the guest lectures were a highlight throughout. Hearing practitioners speak on data, AI usage, and business analytics in industry gave context that no textbook could match - it made the tools we were learning feel immediately relevant.

Why this module matters in the corporate world

Much of what we learned - Git, GitHub, Quarto, web development fundamentals - is not optional in modern workplaces. It’s expected.

  • Git and GitHub are the universal language of collaborative work, not just for developers but increasingly for analysts, data scientists, and business teams who track configuration or documentation under version control.
  • Quarto bridges a gap I didn’t realise existed: the gap between an analyst’s Jupyter notebook and a stakeholder-ready report. In industry, “send me the analysis” means a clean, reproducible document - not a raw notebook.
  • Web development fundamentals - HTML, CSS, SCSS, site configuration - are table-stakes for anyone who wants to publish their work or build internal tools.

Taken together, this module gave me a toolkit I can walk into any corporate environment with and be immediately productive.

Guest lectures - data, AI, and business analytics

The guest lectures were genuinely one of the best parts of the module. Speakers covered how data and AI are being used across industry, and how business analytics is shaping decision-making in real organisations.

A few takeaways that stuck with me:

  • AI in analytics isn’t replacing analysts - it’s amplifying them. The speakers were consistent on this: LLMs and automated tooling handle the grunt work, but framing the right question, sanity-checking results, and communicating insight still needs a human in the loop.
  • Data literacy is a business skill, not a technical one. The most valuable analysts translate between data teams and commercial stakeholders - and that translation work is something no AI tool replaces yet.
  • Portfolios matter in hiring. More than one speaker mentioned that a clean GitHub and a public project page shortcut entire interview rounds. A working site is evidence.
  • Reproducibility is competitive advantage. Teams that can rerun last quarter’s analysis with one command move faster than teams that can’t find last quarter’s spreadsheet.

Those sessions reframed how I think about my own trajectory. The transition from full-stack engineering into business analytics isn’t a side-step - it’s an expansion. The tools are familiar; the audience is new.

Skills I built

Git and GitHub

I’ve used Git before in team settings, but this module made me take solo Git discipline seriously for the first time. Writing small, descriptive commits; maintaining a clean .gitignore; deploying through GitHub Pages rather than uploading files manually - these habits sound small but they separate amateur work from professional work.

Quarto

Easily the biggest new skill. Before this module, my idea of a “portfolio” was a static HTML page or a LinkedIn profile. Quarto turned it into something far more powerful: a living, executable document where every chart regenerates from code, every page is version controlled, and the entire site is reproducible on any machine.

Web development

SCSS variables, Google Fonts, Bootswatch themes, favicons, navbar configuration - none of this was in my day-to-day before. Getting hands-on with a real site (rather than reading about CSS) made the concepts stick in a way passive learning never does.

Writing for a broader audience

Writing the project pages and this reflection pushed me to frame technical work for readers who aren’t deep in the details. That’s a core communication skill in any analytics role - the audience is usually a business stakeholder, not a fellow engineer.

Challenges I overcame

WarningThe friction points
  • YAML indentation. _quarto.yml doesn’t forgive mistakes, and my first silent-failing navbar cost me an hour. Lesson: validate incrementally.
  • GitHub Pages + Jekyll. Forgetting .nojekyll led to a confused evening of “why is my CSS missing?”. Trivial fix once I knew where to look.
  • Balancing polish and substance. It’s tempting to spend a whole day on colour palettes. I set myself a rule: content first, styling last.

What this means for my career

Three lessons I’m taking directly into the next chapter:

  1. Reproducibility as a default. Every analysis under version control, every chart regenerated from code, every decision traceable to the data it came from.
  2. Public presence matters. A polished portfolio and a clean GitHub profile do a lot of the hiring conversation for you, before you’re even in the room.
  3. Communication is half the work. Technical depth alone doesn’t win roles - being able to explain what you did, and why, in clear writing, is what gets remembered.

What I’d do differently

  1. Start the commit discipline from day one rather than catching up mid-project.
  2. Engage with the guest lectures even more actively - ask more questions, follow up with the speakers afterwards.
  3. Treat the portfolio as a long-term asset from the start, not a submission to rush at the end.

Next steps

  • Add a third analytics project using a real public dataset.
  • Wire up GitHub Actions to auto-deploy on every push.
  • Keep the site updated quarterly as coursework and experience grow.

A final thank you

This module genuinely shifted how I think about my own work and how I present it. The combination of practical tooling, strong guest lectures on data, AI, and business analytics, and hands-on sessions made it one of the most useful modules of the MSc so far. The skills built here - Git, Quarto, web development, professional writing - are ones I’ll carry into every role that follows.

The portfolio is a living thing. This reflection will be updated as the site and my career evolve.
